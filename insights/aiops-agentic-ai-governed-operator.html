AIOps, copilots, agents:
a buyer’s map.

Four genuinely different product categories are currently sold under one AI-for-IT banner. Here is the map — and the one question that separates them.

Ask five vendors what "AI for IT operations" means and you'll get five demos that have nothing in common. That's not dishonesty — it's four distinct generations of product sharing one label. The fastest way to cut through it is to ask of each tool: who executes the change?

The four categories

CategoryWhat it doesWho executesLimit
Classic AIOpsCorrelates alerts, reduces noise, predicts anomalies across your monitoring estateA human, elsewhereEnds at a better alert — the work still lands on the rotation
CopilotsChat interfaces that explain errors and suggest commands, queries and TerraformA human, pastingAdvice quality is high; accountability and execution stay manual
Agentic AIAgents that hold credentials and perform multi-step operations end to endThe agentCapability without an external control structure — trust is the ceiling
Governed operatorAgents execute inside a policy engine: RBAC, risk gates, human approval, verification, auditThe agent, inside gatesAutonomy bounded by design — the control plane is the product

Why the fourth column exists

The jump from copilot to agent looks small in a demo and enormous in production. The moment the AI executes, a set of questions becomes non-optional: Who scoped its credentials? What stops a destructive command? Can it approve its own plan? What does the auditor read afterwards? Agentic AI answers those questions with the model's good judgment. A governed operator answers them with architecture — gates the action physically cannot skip.

This is not a theoretical distinction. Prompt injection through logs and tickets, over-scoped credentials, and unauditable chat-driven changes are the three findings every security review of agentic tooling keeps producing. The mitigation for all three lives outside the model: allowlists, separation of duties, bounded runtimes and evidence — the governance layer.

The one-question test: "Show me an autonomous change that was blocked." A copilot has nothing to show. An ungoverned agent has an apology. A governed operator shows you the refused write, the policy that refused it, and the approval that would have let it through.

Where each belongs

  • Classic AIOps earns its keep in large estates drowning in alerts — as a filter in front of humans, not a replacement for them.
  • Copilots are excellent accelerators for engineers who retain full control — and remain the right ceiling for organizations not ready to delegate execution.
  • Ungoverned agents belong in labs and sandboxes, where their blast radius is somebody's weekend project.
  • Governed operators are for production: organizations that want the leverage of autonomy with controls a CISO can sign, an auditor can read, and an operator can trust at 3am.

KYVAR sits squarely in the fourth category — that's the whole design brief. The security architecture shows the gates in detail, and a 30–60 day pilot is how the claim gets tested on your own workload rather than our slides.
