Monitoring that acts:
closing the loop safely.

The industry got very good at detecting problems and stayed mediocre at fixing them. Automatic monitoring is table stakes — the differentiator is what happens in the sixty seconds after the check fails.

Every monitoring pitch ends the same way: a dashboard turns red and someone gets paged. That model has a name — alert-driven operations — and its output is measured in woken humans. The average enterprise added observability tools for a decade and watched MTTR barely move, because detection was never the bottleneck. The bottleneck is the loop between knowing and fixing.

The maturity ladder

Teams climb four rungs, and most are stuck between two and three:

  • 1 — Checks. Ping, port, disk, HTTP 200. You know when something is down, usually after users do.
  • 2 — Alerting with context. Correlated alerts, dashboards, on-call rotations. You know what is down and page fewer people — who then fix everything by hand.
  • 3 — Assisted remediation. Runbooks, scripts, "click to restart". Faster hands, same hands.
  • 4 — Governed auto-remediation. An unhealthy verdict hands the incident to an automated fix — bounded, verified, audited — and a human is woken only when policy wants one.

Why teams stall at rung three

Not because the scripts are hard. Because arming them is scary — and it should be, when the automation has no governance around it. An unbounded auto-fix is an outage generator with good intentions: it flaps, it fixes the symptom on the wrong host, it restarts a database mid-recovery. The prerequisite for rung four isn't better scripts; it's a control structure that makes the automation's worst day survivable:

  • Per-monitor arming. Self-heal is off by default and armed deliberately, one monitor at a time — never a global switch.
  • Bounded remediations. The monitor can trigger exactly the allowlisted action it was given — drain and restart, not "figure it out".
  • Thresholds and cooldowns. Two heals in ten minutes means stop and escalate. Flapping automation must be structurally impossible.
  • Verification closes the loop. The heal re-runs the original check. Not healthy again → rollback and page, with everything it tried in the record.
  • Same rules as humans. A monitor-triggered fix travels the same risk policy, approval gates and audit trail as an operator-requested change. Automation never gets a governance discount.
03:14, replayed: a web tier goes unhealthy. The monitor's verdict hands the incident to its armed remediation: drain from the load balancer, restart, re-register, re-check — 2/2 healthy. The on-call engineer reads about it at 09:00 in the audit trail, with the verification attached. That is the entire pitch of governed Day 2 operations.

Arming your first monitor

Pick the workload where the remediation is boring and the baseline is measurable — a stateless web tier is the classic. Run the monitor unarmed for two weeks and review what it would have done. If the verdicts hold up, arm that one monitor with its one bounded fix, and measure: incidents resolved before user impact, pages avoided, zero ungoverned changes. Then repeat, one monitor at a time.
