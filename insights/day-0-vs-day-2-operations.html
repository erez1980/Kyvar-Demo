Day 0, Day 1, Day 2:
what each one means.

Day 0 is designing, Day 1 is going live and Day 2 is running the system for years. Here is what each phase covers, and why the handoff to Day 2 is where drift and 3am pages come from.

The "day" vocabulary comes from the operations world and it is blunt on purpose. Day 0 is everything before go-live: design and provisioning — turning intent into real infrastructure. Day 1 is the go-live itself: configuration, integration, hardening. Day 2 is everything after, for years: monitoring, incident response, patching, scaling, recovery. Day 2 is where infrastructure spends 99% of its life — and where almost all of its cost and risk live.

Some teams fold Day 0 and Day 1 together as "build", and the exact line moves between organizations. The useful distinction is the one between building a system and running it.

Day 0 vs Day 1

Day 0 produces a plan: the architecture, the sizing, and the code that describes the target state. Nothing is running yet, so mistakes are cheap. Day 1 turns that plan into a live system: resources get provisioned, software is installed and configured, integrations are wired up and traffic is cut over. Day 1 is short but risky, because it is the first time the design meets real networks, real identities and real data.

A simple test: if you can still change your mind by editing a document or a pull request, you are in Day 0. If changing your mind means touching something that exists, you are in Day 1 or later.

What are Day 2 operations?

Day 2 operations are everything needed to keep a system healthy, secure and affordable after go-live. That covers monitoring and alert handling, incident response, patching and vulnerability fixes, scaling, certificate and secret rotation, backup and restore tests, drift correction, cost control and audit evidence. Day 2 has no end date, so it is where most of a system's cost and risk sit, and it is the part of IT operations that automation has served least well.

The split nobody chose

Almost every organization runs Day 0 and Day 2 on different stacks. Day 0 belongs to Terraform, pipelines and a platform team; Day 2 belongs to monitoring tools, ticket queues and an ops rotation. Each side has its own inventory, its own permissions model and its own idea of what "the truth" is.

That split creates three predictable failure modes:

  • Drift. Day 2 fixes happen directly on the infrastructure — a hotfix here, a security-group tweak there — and never flow back into the Day 0 definitions. Within months, the code describes an estate that no longer exists.
  • Tribal knowledge. The remediation for the Tuesday-night failure lives in one engineer's head or a stale wiki page. The build knowledge and the run knowledge are held by different people who rarely trade notes.
  • Double governance. Provisioning changes get code review and approvals; a 3am production fix gets a shrug. The riskiest changes — live ones, under pressure — travel the least-governed path.
The uncomfortable rule: the more urgent a change is, the less governance it gets. Day 2 incidents are exactly when your controls matter most — and exactly when most organizations abandon them.

What one pipeline changes

The alternative is structural: run Day 0 and Day 2 through the same governed pipeline. The same intent-to-action path — request, scope, risk classification, approval, bounded execution, verification, audit — whether the request is "provision a staging instance" or "patch the OpenSSH vulnerability on web-prod-1, one instance at a time".

When both days share one pipeline:

  • The 3am fix carries the same risk policy and leaves the same evidence as a planned change — urgency no longer buys a governance bypass.
  • One inventory serves both: what was built is what is monitored, and a fix lands on the same asset record the provisioner created.
  • Remediation knowledge becomes an executable, reviewable artifact instead of tribal memory — because heals are runs, and runs are recorded.

A quick self-assessment

Five questions expose the size of your handoff gap:

  • Can you trace last week's production hotfix to an approval and a diff?
  • Does your monitoring tool know which Terraform module built the thing it's alerting on?
  • If your best ops engineer left tomorrow, would their remediations leave with them?
  • How many changes last quarter happened outside any pipeline?
  • Is the answer to "what's running in production?" the same in two different tools?

If those answers make you uncomfortable, the fix isn't another tool on each side of the wall — it's removing the wall. Common questions

What is the difference between Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2?

Day 0 is design and build planning, Day 1 is deployment and go-live, and Day 2 is ongoing operation: monitoring, incidents, patching, scaling and recovery for the rest of the system's life.

What does Day 2 operations mean?

Day 2 operations means running a system after it goes live: keeping it healthy, secure and within budget through monitoring, incident response, patching, scaling, backups and compliance work.

What is the difference between Day 0 and Day 1?

Day 0 produces the design and the infrastructure code, before anything runs. Day 1 provisions, configures and cuts over the real system. Day 0 changes are edits to a plan; Day 1 changes touch something that exists.

What is Day 2 automation?

Day 2 automation handles recurring operational work without a person doing each step: restarting a failed service, patching a vulnerable host, rotating a certificate. It is safe in production only when each action is bounded, approved where risk requires it, verified and recorded.

Why does automation break between Day 0 and Day 2?

Because the two phases usually run on different tools and teams. Fixes made during Day 2 rarely flow back into the Day 0 code, which causes drift, and urgent changes often skip the review that planned changes get.
