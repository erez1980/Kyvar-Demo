Day 0 vs Day 2:
the handoff that hurts.

The industry split building and running infrastructure into different tools, different teams and different truths. That split is where drift, tribal knowledge and 3am pages come from.

The "day" vocabulary comes from the operations world and it is blunt on purpose. Day 0 is everything before go-live: design and provisioning — turning intent into real infrastructure. Day 1 is the go-live itself: configuration, integration, hardening. Day 2 is everything after, for years: monitoring, incident response, patching, scaling, recovery. Day 2 is where infrastructure spends 99% of its life — and where almost all of its cost and risk live.

The split nobody chose

Almost every organization runs Day 0 and Day 2 on different stacks. Day 0 belongs to Terraform, pipelines and a platform team; Day 2 belongs to monitoring tools, ticket queues and an ops rotation. Each side has its own inventory, its own permissions model and its own idea of what "the truth" is.

That split creates three predictable failure modes:

  • Drift. Day 2 fixes happen directly on the infrastructure — a hotfix here, a security-group tweak there — and never flow back into the Day 0 definitions. Within months, the code describes an estate that no longer exists.
  • Tribal knowledge. The remediation for the Tuesday-night failure lives in one engineer's head or a stale wiki page. The build knowledge and the run knowledge are held by different people who rarely trade notes.
  • Double governance. Provisioning changes get code review and approvals; a 3am production fix gets a shrug. The riskiest changes — live ones, under pressure — travel the least-governed path.
The uncomfortable rule: the more urgent a change is, the less governance it gets. Day 2 incidents are exactly when your controls matter most — and exactly when most organizations abandon them.

What one pipeline changes

The alternative is structural: run Day 0 and Day 2 through the same governed pipeline. The same intent-to-action path — request, scope, risk classification, approval, bounded execution, verification, audit — whether the request is "provision a staging instance" or "patch the OpenSSH vulnerability on web-prod-1, one instance at a time".

When both days share one pipeline:

  • The 3am fix carries the same risk policy and leaves the same evidence as a planned change — urgency no longer buys a governance bypass.
  • One inventory serves both: what was built is what is monitored, and a fix lands on the same asset record the provisioner created.
  • Remediation knowledge becomes an executable, reviewable artifact instead of tribal memory — because heals are runs, and runs are recorded.

A quick self-assessment

Five questions expose the size of your handoff gap:

  • Can you trace last week's production hotfix to an approval and a diff?
  • Does your monitoring tool know which Terraform module built the thing it's alerting on?
  • If your best ops engineer left tomorrow, would their remediations leave with them?
  • How many changes last quarter happened outside any pipeline?
  • Is the answer to "what's running in production?" the same in two different tools?

If those answers make you uncomfortable, the fix isn't another tool on each side of the wall — it's removing the wall. That's the design premise behind KYVAR's architecture: one control plane where building and operating are the same governed motion.
