For lean IT teams

Fewer alerts. Fewer night pages.
Fixes that wait for a person.

A mid-size IT team cannot staff a NOC. KYVAR folds duplicate alerts into one incident, explains the likely cause, and runs the known fix only where you armed it and policy allows.

The problem

Every tool can alert. Nobody has time to fix.

A team of a few engineers runs cloud, virtualization, servers and the service desk. A probe, a monitor and a ticket all report the same server, a flapping detector pages twice an hour, and half the night pages end with the same restart someone has done twenty times before. Detection is not the bottleneck. The time between knowing and fixing is.

The approach

From an alert to a verified fix.

One incident, not five alerts

A probe, a monitor and a ticket about the same asset become one incident. Repeating alerts fold into it, and flapping detectors are named with a suggested threshold.

A cause, with evidence

Investigation produces a root-cause report with confidence and evidence, and similar past incidents. Telemetry from Datadog or Splunk is queried at investigation time, not copied into KYVAR.

Monitors that may act

Armed per monitor and off by default, an unhealthy verdict can launch a bounded fix, a named runbook or a workflow, behind thresholds, cooldowns and approvals.

Proof it worked

The original check runs again after the fix. Recurring-problem analysis shows which assets keep failing and the time lost to them, and the impact map shows what else stops if an asset goes down.

Architecture

Keep your monitoring. Add the loop.

KYVAR takes alerts from the tools you already run, including CloudWatch, Azure Monitor, Google Cloud, New Relic, Zabbix, Elastic, Sentry, Alertmanager and Opsgenie, and its own probes and monitors. A runtime close to the target, in a cloud account or inside your network, carries out the work over outbound HTTPS only. See the architecture page.

Security

Automation gets no governance discount.

Off until armed

Self-healing is armed one monitor at a time, never with a global switch, and an emergency stop halts all of it.

The same gates as a person

A monitor-triggered fix travels the same risk policy, approvals and audit as an operator’s change. Change requests started by an alert wait for a person.

Loops are bounded

Thresholds and cooldowns stop a fix that repeats. High-risk real writes are refused outright.

An example

A stopped server, handled before morning.

An illustrative run:

  1. A monitor finds a cloud instance stopped. A probe on the same server fails too, and both become one incident.
  2. The monitor is armed with a start-or-reboot remediation, inside its cooldown and threshold.
  3. KYVAR reads the live instance state, starts it, and runs the original health check again: healthy.
  4. The incident is resolved on the record with every step, and the engineer reads it in the morning instead of being woken.
Frequently asked

Questions buyers ask first.

Does KYVAR replace our monitoring tools?

No. It takes alerts from the tools you already run and queries telemetry from Datadog or Splunk at investigation time. What it adds is the governed step from an alert to a verified fix.

Can auto-remediation run without a person approving it?

Only where you armed it, one monitor at a time, and only within risk policy. It is off by default, high-risk real writes are refused, and change requests that an alert starts wait for human approval.

How does KYVAR reduce alert noise?

Alerts about the same asset from a probe, a monitor and a ticket are correlated into one incident, repeating alerts fold into it, and flapping detectors are named with a suggested threshold.

What stops a fix from looping?

Every armed remediation has thresholds and cooldowns, and the original check runs again after the fix. A fix that does not restore health is not repeated without limit.

Which alert sources are supported?

CloudWatch, Azure Monitor, Google Cloud, New Relic, Zabbix, Elastic, Sentry, Alertmanager and Opsgenie, plus KYVAR’s own probes and monitors.
